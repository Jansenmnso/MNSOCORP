1. The Octet Rule (Oktetregelen)
Core Principle
Atoms follow the octet rule because a full outer shell (valence shell) gives the lowest energy and greatest stability. Atoms "want" to have 8 electrons in their outermost shell.
How Atoms Achieve This
• Give away electrons → become cations (positive ions) • Take electrons from others → become anions (negative ions) • Share electrons with other atoms → covalent bonding When the outermost shell is full, an atom is stable and unreactive.
2. Molecules & States of Matter
What is a Molecule?
A molecule consists of at least 2 atoms bonded together. It can be: • An elemental molecule (e.g., O₂) • A chemical compound (e.g., H₂O)
Polar vs. Nonpolar Molecules
Polar molecule: One side is (+) and the other is (−). Uneven charge distribution. Often dissolves in water. Nonpolar molecule: Even/symmetric charge distribution. Does NOT dissolve well in water. → "Like dissolves like"
Stability & Reactions
Atoms react in order to achieve a full outer shell. Noble gases already have full outer shells → very unreactive. Chemical reactions involve: atoms rearranging, and bonds breaking and forming.
3. Ionic Bonding (Ionebinding)
Definition
Occurs most often between a metal and a non-metal. Electrons are transferred from one atom to another.
Ion Types
• Positive ion = Cation (+): loses an electron • Negative ion = Anion (−): gains electrons
4. Covalent Bonding (Kovalent binding)
Definition
Occurs between non-metals. Atoms share one or more electron pairs → forms a molecule. Atoms achieve a full outer shell (valence shell) through sharing. The bond holds the atoms together in a molecule.
Types of Covalent Bonds
• Single bond (—): one shared electron pair • Double bond (=): two shared electron pairs • Triple bond (≡): three shared electron pairs
Characteristics
• Every two shared electrons = one bond • Often lower melting point than ionic bonds • Conducts electricity poorly (most cases) • Strong within a molecule, but weak between molecules
5. Hydrogen Bonding (Hydrogenbinding)
Definition
A weak bond between a hydrogen atom and an electronegative atom. The hydrogen atom must be covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom such as oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), or fluorine (F).
Effect
Gives hydrogen a partial positive charge and the other atom a partial negative charge. → Responsible for many of water's unique properties (e.g., high boiling point, surface tension).
6. Intermolecular Forces (Dipole & London)
Dipole-Dipole Forces
Exist between polar molecules. Molecule has a positive end (+) and a negative end (−) → uneven electron distribution. Positive and negative ends attract each other. • Stronger than London forces but weaker than hydrogen bonds • Affect boiling and melting points • Important for solubility in water
London Dispersion Forces
Exist between ALL molecules. Caused by temporary fluctuations in electron distribution → creates temporary dipoles. Weakest of the intermolecular forces.
Intermolecular Forces (General)
Forces that act between molecules (not within them). Weaker than metallic, covalent, and ionic bonds.
7. Metallic Bonding (Metallbinding)
Definition
Occurs between metal atoms. Positive metal ions sit in a lattice surrounded by free-moving (delocalized) electrons.
Properties
• Electrons are delocalized (move freely throughout the lattice) • Conducts electricity and heat • Can be shaped/formed (e.g., hammering, rolling) • Has metallic luster • Varying hardness and strength (hard and soft metals)
8. Naming Compounds (Stoff og Regler)
Greek Prefixes
1 – mono   2 – di   3 – tri   4 – tetra   5 – penta 6 – hexa   7 – hepta   8 – octa   9 – nona   10 – deca
Naming Covalent (Non-metal + Non-metal) Compounds
Use prefixes to indicate the number of each atom. The least electronegative element comes first. Exception: in carbon chemistry, carbon (C) often comes first (e.g., CO₂). Examples: NO → Nitrogen monoxide N₂O → Dinitrogen oxide NO₂ → Nitrogen dioxide N₂O₅ → Dinitrogen pentoxide SF₆ → Sulfur hexafluoride CS₂ → Carbon disulfide
Naming Ionic (Metal + Non-metal) Compounds
Write the metal first (it keeps its name). The non-metal gets a modified "-ide" ending (e.g., oxygen → oxide, chlorine → chloride). NaCl = Sodium chloride LiF = Lithium fluoride Na₂S = Sodium sulfide MgCl₂ = Magnesium chloride (NOT magnesium dichloride — prefixes not used for ionic compounds) Ca₃N₂ = Calcium nitride For metals that can form multiple charge states, use Roman numerals: FeO = Iron(II) oxide Fe₂O₃ = Iron(III) oxide
9. The Periodic Table (Periodesystemet)
Groups (Grupper)
18 vertical columns. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties and the same number of electrons in their outermost shell.
Periods (Perioder)
Horizontal rows. Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells (energy levels). Example – Period 2: Lithium → Neon.
Metals / Non-metals / Metalloids
Classified by chemical and physical properties, especially ability to conduct electricity/heat. Metals are on the left, non-metals on the right, metalloids (halvmetaller) in between.
Electron Configuration
The number of valence dots on a Lewis dot diagram = the group number. The atomic number = position on the periodic table. Example: Na has 11 electrons → electron configuration 2, 8, 1 (3 shells).
10. Atom Structure
Parts of an Atom
• Nucleus (Kjerne): contains protons (+) and neutrons (neutral) • Electron shells (Elektronskall): electrons orbit the nucleus in energy levels • Each shell holds a maximum number of electrons (Shell 1: 2, Shell 2: 8, Shell 3: 8)
Common Element Symbols
N = Nitrogen    C = Carbon (Karbon)    H = Hydrogen Fe = Iron (Jern)    Al = Aluminum    I = Iodine (Jod) Cu = Copper (Kobber)    Mg = Magnesium    He = Helium
11. Bond Strength Comparison
Strong Bonds (Intramolecular)
Ionic bond ≈ Covalent bond > Metallic bond
Weak Forces (Intermolecular)
Hydrogen bond > Dipole-dipole > London dispersion
